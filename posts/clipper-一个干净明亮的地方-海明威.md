## 一个干净明亮的地方

**海明威 著**

![](https://picx.zhimg.com/v2-2c31fd7fdc38b7b77e6d8aa17644c12d_r.jpg)

**ZzzzzzzSlumber 译**

很晚了，餐馆里人都走光了，只有一个老人还坐在灯光下树叶的阴影里。白天街上尘土飞扬，但是到了晚上露水就把尘土压了下来。老人喜欢坐到很晚，因为他耳聋而现在很安静，他能感到夜晚与白天的不同之处。餐馆里的两位侍者知道这老人有点小醉，虽说他是个好主顾，可他们知道，要是他喝过头了，他会钱都不付就走掉，所以他们一直在盯着他。

“上周他自杀了，” 一个侍者说。

“为什么？”

“他已经绝望了。”

“怎么回事？”

“没事。”

“你怎么知道没事？”

“他有的是钱。”

他们一起坐在桌旁，桌子挨着餐馆门口边的墙。他们望着餐馆外的露台，桌子空空荡荡，只有老人坐在树叶的阴影里，树在随风轻摇。一个女孩和一个士兵路过大街。街灯照在他衣领处的铜质号码上。女孩没有蒙头 [1]，在他身旁匆匆行路。

“警卫队会来他接的，” 一个侍者说。

“如果他拿到了他要的东西那算得了什么？”

“他还是赶紧从街上溜走为好。警卫队会拿下他的。他们五分钟前就路过这里。”

坐在阴影里的老人拿酒杯敲了敲茶托。年轻的侍者走了过去。

“你要来点什么？”

老人看着他。“再来杯白兰地，” 他说。

“你会喝醉的，” 侍者说。老人看着他。侍者走了。

“他整晚都会待在这里，” 他对同事说。“我困了。我就没在三点前睡上觉过。他上周就该死了。”

侍者从餐馆里的柜台里取出白兰地和另一个茶托，大步走了出来，到老人的桌子边上。他放下茶托，倒了满满一杯白兰地。

“你上周就该死了，” 他对这个耳聋的人说。老人用手指示意。“再加点，” 他说。侍者往酒杯里继续倒，直到酒满出来沿着杯脚向下流进了堆顶的那只茶托。“谢谢你，” 老人说。侍者把酒瓶拿回餐馆去。他又和他同事坐在桌旁。

“现在他醉了，” 他说。

“他每天晚上都喝醉。”

“他自杀是为了什么？”

“我怎么知道。”

“他怎么做的？”

“他用绳子上吊。”

“谁把他救下来的？”

“他侄女。”

“他为什么这么做？”

“为了他的灵魂。”

“他有多少钱？”

“他有很多钱。”

“他一定八十岁了。”

“应该说，他八十多了。”

“我希望他回家。我就没在三点前睡上觉过。那样的睡觉时间算什么啊？”

“他不睡是因为他喜欢。”

“他孤单，我又不孤单。我有老婆在床上等我。”

“他以前也有老婆。”

“对他来说现在有老婆不是好事。”

“说不定。他有老婆也许会更好受一点。”

“他侄女会照顾他的。”

“我知道。你说过是她把他救下来的。”

“我可不想那么老。老人个个都是讨厌的东西。”

“并非总是如此。这个老人很干净。他喝酒时从来不洒出来。即使是现在，他喝醉了。你看。”

“我不想看他。我希望他会回家。他根本不关心那些要干活的人。”

老人隔着酒杯看向露台对面，又回过来看侍者。

“再来杯白兰地，” 他说，指着他的酒杯。那个心急的侍者来了。

“没了，” 他说，没有组织语言，这是傻瓜对醉汉或外国人说话的口吻，“今晚没了。现在打烊了。”

“再来一杯，” 老人说。

“不，没了，” 侍者用毛巾擦桌沿并摇头。

老人站了起来，慢慢数着茶托，从口袋里取出一只装硬币的皮质钱包付了酒钱，留了半个比塞塔 [2] 的小费。

那个侍者看着他顺着大街走去，一个很老的人摇摇晃晃却充满尊严地行走着。

“你为什么不让他待着喝酒？” 那个不急的侍者问道。他们正在关百叶窗。“两点半都没到。”

“我要回家睡觉。”

“一个小时算得了什么？”

“与他相比对我来说更重要。”

“一个小时就是一个小时。”

“你怎么说话像个老头一样。他可以买一瓶回家喝。”

“不一样的。”

“是不一样。” 有老婆的侍者表示同意。他不希望不公平，他只是很急。

“你呢？你不怕不到平时的时间就回家吗？”

“你是想侮辱我？”

“不，老兄，玩笑而已。”

“不，” 那个着急的侍者拉下金属百叶窗后边站起来边说，“我有信心。我完全有信心。”

“你有青春、信心，还有工作，” 那个年长的侍者说，“你什么都有了。”

“那你缺什么？”

“除了工作什么都缺。”

“我有的你都有。”

“不。我从来没有信心，我也不年轻。”

“来吧。别废话，锁门了。”

“我是那种喜欢在餐馆待到很晚的人，” 年长的侍者说。

“和那些不想上床睡觉的人一起。和那些在夜里需要光的人一起。”

“我要回家睡觉去了。”

“我们是两种不同的人，” 年长的侍者说。他现在已经穿好衣服要回家了。“这不仅仅是年轻和信心的问题，虽然那些东西很美。我每天晚上都不愿打烊因为说不定有人需要这个餐馆。”

“老兄，开通宵的酒店有的是。”

“你不懂。这里是一个干净愉快的餐馆。这里光照充足。灯光明亮而且，现在还有树叶的阴影。”

“晚安，” 年轻的侍者说。

“晚安，”另一个说。关闭电灯后他继续自言自语。当然要有光，但也要干净愉快。你不要音乐。你肯定不要音乐。你也不能充满尊严地站在吧台前，虽然那是这几个小时能够提供的一切。他怕什么？那不是害怕或者恐惧。那是他再清楚不过的虚无。那仅仅是虚无而已，人也是虚无。那只不过是虚无，他需要也只不过是光和某种干净整洁的氛围。有些人生活在其中却从来没有感觉到它，但他知道一切都是虚无然后是虚无和虚无然后是虚无 [3]。我们在虚无中的虚无，因你的名被虚无化。愿你的国变得虚无，愿你的旨意在虚无中化为虚无，如同行在虚无之中。我们日用的虚无，在此虚无中赐给我们，把我们的虚无变得虚无，如同我们把人的虚无变得虚无。不把我们虚无化为虚无，救我们脱离虚无；还是虚无。万福虚无，充满虚无，虚无与你同在。[4] 他微笑着，站在酒吧前，那儿有台闪闪发光的蒸气式咖啡机。

“你要什么？” 酒吧招待问道。

“虚无。”

“又来了个神经病，” 酒吧招待说着转过身去。

“来一小杯，” 侍者说。

酒吧招待倒了一杯给他。

“灯很亮很愉快但吧台不光洁，” 侍者说。

酒吧招待看着他没有回应。要想谈话的话也太晚了。

“你要再来一小杯吗？” 酒吧招待问道。

“不用，谢了，” 侍者说完就出去了。他不喜欢酒吧和酒店。一个干净明亮的餐馆是个非常不一样的地方。现在，他没再多想就要回家到他的房间里去。他会躺在床上，最后天亮了，他就能睡了。毕竟，他对自己说，很可能是失眠而已。许多人一定都失眠。

## A Clean Well-Lighted Place

**by Ernest Hemingway**

It was very late and everyone had left the café except an old man who sat in the shadow the leaves of the tree made against the electric light. In the day time the street was dusty, but at night the dew settled the dust and the old man liked to sit late because he was deaf and now at night it was quiet and he felt the difference. The two waiters inside the café knew that the old man was a little drunk, and while he was a good client they knew that if he became too drunk he would leave without paying, so they kept watch on him.

“Last week he tried to commit suicide,” one waiter said.

“Why?”

“He was in despair.”

“What about?”

“Nothing.”

“How do you know it was nothing?”

“He has plenty of money.”

They sat together at a table that was close against the wall near the door of the cafe and looked at the terrace where the tables were all empty except where the old man sat in the shadow of the leaves of the tree that moved slightly in the wind. A girl and a soldier went by in the street. The street light shone on the brass number on his collar. The girl wore no head covering and hurried beside him.

“The guard will pick him up,” one waiter said.

“What does it matter if he gets what he’s after?”

“He had better get off the street now. The guard will get him. They went by five minutes ago.”

The old man sitting in the shadow rapped on his saucer with his glass. The younger waiter went over to him.

“What do you want?”

The old man looked at him. “Another brandy,” he said.

“You'll be drunk,” the waiter said. The old man looked at him. The waiter went away.

“He'll stay all night,” he said to his colleague. “I'm sleepy now. I never get into bed before three o'clock. He should have killed himself last week.”

The waiter took the brandy bottle and another saucer from the counter inside the café and marched out to the old man's table. He put down the saucer and poured the glass full of brandy.

“You should have killed yourself last week,” he said to the deaf man. The old man motioned with his finger. “A little more,” he said. The waiter poured on into the glass so that the brandy slopped over and ran down the stem into the top saucer of the pile. “Thank you,” the old man said. The waiter took the bottle back inside the café. He sat down at the table with his colleague again.

“He's drunk now,” he said.

“He's drunk every night.”

“What did he want to kill himself for?”

“How should I know.”

“How did he do it?”

“He hung himself with a rope.”

“Who cut him down?”

“His niece.”

“Why did he do it?”

“Fear for his soul.”

“How much money has he got?”

“He’s got plenty.”

“He must be eighty years old.”

“Anyway I should say he was eighty.”

“I wish he would go home. I never get to bed before three o'clock. What kind of hour is that to go to bed?”

“He stays up because he likes it.”

“He's lonely. I'm not lonely. I have a wife waiting in bed for me.”

“He had a wife once too.”

“A wife would be no good to him now.”

“You can't tell. He might be better with a wife.”

“His niece looks after him. You said she cut him down.”

“I know.” “I wouldn't want to be that old. An old man is a nasty thing.”

“Not always. This old man is clean. He drinks without spilling. Even now, drunk. Look at him.”

“I don't want to look at him. I wish he would go home. He has no regard for those who must work.”

The old man looked from his glass across the square, then over at the waiters.

“Another brandy,” he said, pointing to his glass. The waiter who was in a hurry came over.

“Finished,” he said, speaking with that omission of syntax stupid people employ when talking to drunken people or foreigners. “No more tonight. Close now.”

“Another,” said the old man.

“No. Finished.” The waiter wiped the edge of the table with a towel and shook his head.

The old man stood up, slowly counted the saucers, took a leather coin purse from his pocket and paid for the drinks, leaving half a peseta tip. The waiter watched him go down the street, a very old man walking unsteadily but with dignity.

“Why didn't you let him stay and drink?” the unhurried waiter asked. They were putting up the shutters. “It is not half-past two.”

“I want to go home to bed.”

“What is an hour?”

“More to me than to him.”

“An hour is the same.”

“You talk like an old man yourself. He can buy a bottle and drink at home.”

“It's not the same.”

“No, it is not,” agreed the waiter with a wife. He did not wish to be unjust. He was only in a hurry.

“And you? You have no fear of going home before your usual hour?”

“Are you trying to insult me?”

“No, hombre, only to make a joke.”

“No,” the waiter who was in a hurry said, rising from pulling down the metal shutters. “I have confidence. I am all confidence.”

“You have youth, confidence, and a job,” the older waiter said. “You have everything.”

“And what do you lack?”

“Everything but work.”

“You have everything I have.”

“No. I have never had confidence and I am not young.”

“Come on. Stop talking nonsense and lockup.”

“I am of those who like to stay late at the café,” the older waiter said.

“With all those who do not want to go to bed. With all those who need a light for the night.”

“I want to go home and into bed.”

“We are of two different kinds,” the older waiter said. He was now dressed to go home. “It is not only a question of youth and confidence although those things are very beautiful. Each night I am reluctant to close up because there may be some one who needs the café.”

“Hombre, there are bodegas open all night long.”

“You do not understand. This is a clean and pleasant cafe. It is well lighted. The light is very good and also, now, there are shadows of the leaves.”

“Good night,” said the younger waiter.

“Good night,” the other said. Turning off the electric light he continued the conversation with himself, It was the light of course but it is necessary that the place be clean and pleasant. You do not want music. Certainly you do not want music. Nor can you stand before a bar with dignity although that is all that is provided for these hours. What did he fear? It was not a fear or dread, It was a nothing that he knew too well. It was all a nothing and a man was a nothing too. It was only that and light was all it needed and a certain cleanness and order. Some lived in it and never felt it but he knew it all was nada y pues nada y nada y pues nada. Our nada who art in nada, nada be thy name thy kingdom nada thy will be nada in nada as it is in nada. Give us this nada our daily nada and nada us our nada as we nada our nadas and nada us not into nada but deliver us from nada; pues nada. Hail nothing full of nothing, nothing is with thee. He smiled and stood before a bar with a shining steam pressure coffee machine.

“What's yours?” asked the barman.

“Nada.”

“Otro loco mas,” said the barman and turned away.

“A little cup,” said the waiter.

The barman poured it for him.

“The light is very bright and pleasant but the bar is unpolished,” the waiter said.

The barman looked at him but did not answer. It was too late at night for conversation.

“You want another copita?” the barman asked.

“No, thank you,” said the waiter and went out. He disliked bars and bodegas. A clean, well-lighted cafe was a very different thing. Now, without thinking further, he would go home to his room. He would lie in the bed and finally, with daylight, he would go to sleep. Afterall, he said to himself, it's probably only insomnia. Many must have it.
